## 0. Short answer

1. **Today Letflow is visible, not open source.** The repo is public but has no licence, so everyone else has no right
   to use it. Decide this on purpose, soon: either make the repo private until the plan below is done, or publish the
   licence. (Owner question Q1. Making it private is reversible; leaving it as is, is a silent decision.)
2. **Recommendation: open core.** A public **Community Edition (CE)** = the platform core + the generic, small
   modules + the design system, under **Apache-2.0**. A closed **Corporate Edition (CE+, name open)** = the CE plus
   closed *module packages* and a small set of *enterprise features* (per-company sign-in, extra audit, signed
   updates, support). Anything customer-specific (exam app, procurement, demo seed) stays closed or private.
3. **Do not open anything before the clean-up (section 5).** The history of the repo has never been audited for
   secrets, and `handoffs/` alone holds 3,864 tracked files. The safe route is a **new, squashed public repository**,
   not flipping the old one with its history.
4. **The hardest engineering part is that the core is one 194k-line block with enterprise features inside it.**
   The split of those features out of the core is the biggest item (section 7, stage 2).

## 2. Licence: options and recommendation

| Option | For | Against | Fits when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Apache-2.0** (recommended for CE) | Permissive, with an express patent grant (section 3) and a copyright grant that allows use, modification and redistribution (section 2); conditions on redistributors are attribution, NOTICE and change notices (section 4); no warranty (section 7; section 5 is about contributions) — [Apache-2.0 text](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0). Permissive licences are commonly easier for companies to accept, but each company decides for itself (an expectation, not a guarantee). Matches the dependencies (Apache-2.0 and MIT dominate the direct dependencies, per the facts file); nothing in that list blocks it | Anyone, including a cloud vendor or a competitor, can host or resell the CE without giving anything back | The aim is adoption and a community; money comes from the corporate edition, hosting and support |
| **AGPL-3.0** | Obligations (primary text: [AGPL-3.0](https://www.gnu.org/licenses/agpl-3.0.html), section 13): a party who *modifies* the program and lets users interact with it remotely over a network must prominently offer those users the Corresponding Source of that modified version. This is an offer of source to the remote users, not a duty to publish every hosting change to the world, and running an unmodified copy adds no such duty. Copyleft also applies to conveyed copies (sections 5 and 6). The owner of the copyright can additionally sell a *commercial licence* to those who cannot accept AGPL (dual licensing), but only for code the owner has the right to relicense | Some companies have policies against AGPL, which can reduce adoption (UAT assumption, not measured here). Outside contributions are possible under AGPL itself; a CLA or assignment is needed if we want the right to relicense AGPL-only contributions commercially (for dual licensing). A contribution made under Apache-2.0 is different (section 3) Every dependency must be compatible (the direct ones are; transitive ones are unverified); the guard against a hosted rival is only as strong as our will to enforce it | The main risk is a rival hosting Letflow, and dual licensing is the business model |
| **BUSL-1.1 / source-available** | Strong protection from hosting rivals for a set period, then converts to open source | Primary text: [BUSL-1.1](https://mariadb.com/bsl11/): production use is limited unless the licensor's *Additional Use Grant* allows it, and on the *Change Date* (at most four years after a version's release) the code converts to the stated *Change License*. It is **not open source** by the OSI definition. That communities and some customers react badly and that outside contributions are rare are UAT assumptions, not measured here; the Additional Use Grant text needs a lawyer | The aim is only to be seen and trusted, not a community |

Recommendation: **Apache-2.0 for the CE**, closed commercial licence for the corporate packages. Reasoning: the
platform's value for the owner's pilots is adoption of the modules and the process engine in the Kazakh/Russian-speaking
market; a hosting rival taking the CE is a smaller risk than a licence that stops adoption. If the owner's answer to
Q2 (section 9) is "a hoster copying us is the real fear", the fallback is **AGPL-3.0 with a CLA** (dual licence), not BUSL.
This is a recommendation for the owner and a lawyer to confirm (UAT assumption, to be confirmed by the process owner);
it is not legal advice.

A licence is chosen once per release: code already published under Apache-2.0 stays Apache-2.0 for the people who
got it. So the order matters: choose first, publish second.

## 3. Contributions and trademark

Two different questions, kept apart:
(a) *Using the grant we receive.* Under Apache-2.0 section 5 a contribution submitted to a project is licensed under the
same licence unless stated otherwise. Section 2 grants the right to sublicense, and section 4 allows derivative works to be
distributed under different terms for the derivative's own parts, provided the Apache-2.0 conditions for the included work
are kept. So Apache-2.0 contributions can go into a closed build without a CLA, with attribution, NOTICE and change notices.
(b) *Wider rights than the grant gives*: removing the obligations Apache-2.0 puts on the contribution itself, or obtaining
commercial relicensing rights over contributions made under a licence that does not grant them (for example AGPL-only
contributions). That needs a CLA or copyright assignment.
Source: [Apache-2.0 sections 2, 4 and 5](https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0).

- **DCO** (a `Signed-off-by` line, checked in CI) is a possible start: no paperwork, fits Apache-2.0. (UAT assumption, to be confirmed by the process owner; the choice waits for Q3.)
- **CLA** is needed only for (b). If the owner picks the AGPL dual-licence fallback, a CLA from day one is advisable, because adding it later means asking every earlier contributor.
- **Interim contribution policy: not decided.** Whether the CE takes code from outside, takes bug fixes only, or takes issues only is pending the owner's answer to Q3. Nothing in this note sets it. The files `CONTRIBUTING.md`, `SECURITY.md` (private reporting address) and a code of conduct are proposed (UAT assumption, to be confirmed by the process owner), with their content depending on Q3.
- **Apache-2.0 gives no trademark right** (section 6 of the same text). The name "Letflow" is the working name (the former name RoCo is in the product's `CLAUDE.md`, line 4). A trademark policy is needed; its permissions (for example whether forks may use the name, or "powered by Letflow" is allowed) are **pending the owner's decision Q5**; any wording here is an example only (UAT assumption, to be confirmed by the process owner). **Check the name for clashes before any announcement** (I have not searched trademark registers).
- A `NOTICE` file lists the name and attributions required by the dependencies and fonts.

## 4. How the corporate edition is built from the community one

Principle: **the CE never contains a call to closed code; the corporate edition adds, it does not patch.** (The
boundary check already enforces "modules call only the core and their declared dependencies" — REQ-405.)

1. **Closed module packages.** Exam, procurement and future paid modules live in a private repo, built against the
   public core as a dependency. The module manifest and the per-tenant install/upgrade plan (U1–U4) are the delivery
   mechanism; nothing new is needed for the shape, only a way to load a module that is not in the core's source tree
   (today modules are compiled into the build; see T-1519 for what that means for updates). *Open design item.*
2. **Signed packages (proposal).** The existing manifest hash (`lib/letflow/modules/manifest_hash.ex`) is **not enough**
   to sign: it covers only the declared surface (ids, version, entity types, scope declarations, reference targets, ledger
   registration, write-handler declarations) and explicitly does NOT cover permissions, routes, settings, pack content or
   handler code; its moduledoc says a matching hash is not proof of behaviour (same distinction as in T-1519). A signature over
   it would let an edited package install. The proposal is a **package signature over the whole delivered package**: a signed
   manifest file that lists every delivered file (code, pack content, permissions, routes, settings) with its SHA-256 digest;
   at install the core recomputes each digest from the delivered files and verifies the signature with a public key held in the core,
   and refuses on any mismatch or missing/extra file. The existing manifest hash stays as a separate consistency stamp. I found no
   signature code today, so this is new work. The signing key is a secret handled by reference only (INV-4), never in git.
   Details (format, key rotation, revocation) are a design item for stage 2.
3. **Licence key and feature flags.** A corporate feature is a flag that is off by default (the product rule: new flags
   default off) and turns on only with a licence file for that company, signed like a package. **What happens when a licence expires, is missing, or cannot be read is NOT decided here**: it changes
   customer access to features and data in production. Options for the owner: (i) the feature is switched off, data stays readable;
   (ii) the feature goes read-only with a visible notice; (iii) a grace period, then (i) or (ii); (iv) nothing changes until the
   next upgrade. In every option the question of whether sign-in and read access to the tenant's own data continue must be answered
   explicitly (see Q9). No option is recommended before the owner decides.
4. **Per-company sign-in (F1b).** Today one realm per tenant exists on QA through a provisioner (decision 0072; the
   team goal says QA only, production is not approved). CE: one identity provider configured by the operator. Corporate:
   the multi-realm per-company sign-in and the provisioner contract. The seam is an identity-provider interface in the core
   with the CE implementation public and the realm-per-tenant one closed. Tenant isolation (INV-1, INV-10) must be
   security-reviewed for both implementations.
5. **Audit.** The CE keeps the event store and the base audit (needed for the platform to be correct). Corporate adds
   export/retention/tamper-evidence options on top, through the same seam idea. Which audit parts are "base" is a
   security decision: an audit that is weaker in the CE must not break INV-2/INV-5.
6. **Build.** One CI pipeline for the public repo; a second private repo checks out the public core at a tag, adds the closed packages, runs
   the same tests plus its own. The corporate build is never the source of truth for core code: core fixes land in the public
   repo first.
7. **Tests.** The existing sharded gate keeps working for the CE; closed modules bring their own tests. Permission matrix
   golden files and route-count pins (which collide today between tasks) must be split per edition, or a closed module
   would break the public CI. *Open design item; it is the least visible cost of the split.*

## 5. Clean-up before anything is opened (a checklist, in order)

1. **Freeze**: make the current repo private (or confirm the owner accepts it staying public) until the list is done. [Q1]
2. **Secrets audit of the full history**, with a scanner run over every commit and every branch and tag (for example
   gitleaks and trufflehog; the team's own checks only looked at the working tree). Rotate any credential that ever
   appeared, even if deleted later. The finding list is reviewed by the security reviewer. Pre-condition for everything else.
3. **Documents review**: a human list of every file under `docs/`, `handoffs/`, `project/`, runbooks and UAT fixtures with a verdict
   public / rewrite / closed. Check for customer names, QA hostnames, accounts, prices, e-mails, internal decisions (D-numbers).
   `handoffs/` (3,864 tracked files) and `state/` do not go public.
4. **Third-party licence scan** in CI, producing a licence report per release:
   Elixir (already 34 of 34 checked, no copyleft: re-run as a CI step); npm with the **full transitive tree** (a scanner such as
   `license-checker`); Flutter/pub for all 84 packages by reading each package's LICENSE file (pub.dev does not expose a licence field);
   Docker/base images and the Keycloak/nginx/PostgreSQL deployment files (used, not linked: attribute only).
   Proposed rule (UAT assumption, to be confirmed by the process owner and a lawyer): no GPL/AGPL/SSPL/BUSL dependency linked into the CE; LGPL/MPL/EPL need a case-by-case note.
5. **Fonts and assets**: the fonts (Geologica, IBM Plex Mono, Onest) ship with OFL texts and a `LICENSES.md` in
   `web/src/fonts/` — good; confirm those files match the shipped font files and that icons, images and `web/public/brand`,
   `design-system` folders have a licence or are original. OFL allows redistribution but not selling the font alone and forbids reusing
   a Reserved Font Name for a modified font. Brand files are excluded from the Apache grant.
6. **Engine isolation**: confirm the public repo has no reference to the tasks-engine repository content (`team.py`, `project/` instance files, `.claude/agents` role files) — role files in `.claude/` are our process, review before publishing.
7. **Add the legal files**: `LICENSE` (Apache-2.0), `NOTICE`, per-file SPDX headers (checked by a lint), `package.json`/`mix.exs`/`pubspec.yaml` licence fields, `CONTRIBUTING.md`, `SECURITY.md`, trademark policy.
8. **Security posture review (publication gate)**: an open repo makes the code readable to attackers; open security findings and the QA-only
   caveats (provisioning not approved for production) are fixed or documented first. Its completion, signed by the security reviewer,
   is an explicit prerequisite of item 9.
9. **Publish a fresh repository** with a squashed history (or a history filtered by the audit), not the old repo. The old one
   stays private as the archive. Allowed only after items 2–8 are done.

## 6. Risks to name

- A tenant-data bug found by the public is a real incident; the CE needs a published security-reporting path and someone who answers it.
- Two editions double test and release work for a small team; the split must not slow the pilots (BilimBaga, Qoimeks), which come first in the portfolio.
- The core split (stage 2) touches tenant-data paths: each step is security-reviewed (INV-1, INV-10).
- If outside contributions arrive under AGPL-only terms without a CLA, commercial relicensing of them is not possible; Apache-2.0 contributions can be used in a closed build under the grant (section 3).

## 8. Alternatives considered

- **Everything stays closed** (private repo, no CE): simplest, no risk of exposure; loses adoption, outside review, and the trust argument for a platform that holds tenants' data. Valid if the business model is only custom projects for pilots.
- **Everything open, services only**: strongest community; no product revenue except support/hosting; hard for a small team.
- **Open only the modules (not the core)**: useless without the core to run them.
- **Source-available whole product (BUSL)**: cheapest in engineering (no split needed), gives visibility; see section 2 for the cost.
